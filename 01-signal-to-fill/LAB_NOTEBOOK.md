## 2026-11-17 (Tue) · taker orders across the delay grid, dev day

Each decision is replayed at all nine delays on the same path.
- **Result (`results/taker/taker_dev_2026-09-01.json`).** The latency cost is close to zero. ## 2026-11-18 (Wed) · passive orders with a virtual queue marker, dev day
- **Built** `src/s2f/passive.py` (`make passive`, five more tests, fifteen pass). A 0.01 BTC order joins the best price on its own side at decision time plus delay. The queue ahead is the displayed size at arrival. I follow my level with the top of book updates, and with the five level snapshot while a better price sits in front of me. Trades come first, then book updates. Three cancellation rules run on the same path and trades (front, random with seed 20261115, back). A cancel is any drop in displayed size that trades do not explain, and a rise joins behind me. If my price leaves the top five I count the order as unfilled from then on. 8,425 decisions, nine delays, same decisions as the taker leg.
- **Result (`results/passive/passive_dev_2026-09-01.json`).** Fill probability within 10 s barely moves with delay and the rule moves it a lot. At 1 ms it is 68.3% (front), 54.9% (random) and 46.7% (back). At 500 ms it is 68.3%, 55.1% and 46.7%. Slope of fill probability on log delay per rule, with 95% block bootstrap intervals. Front 0.00018 (negative 0.00068 to 0.00103), random 0.00027 (negative 0.00049 to 0.00108), back negative 0.00009 (negative 0.00075 to 0.00059). All three cover zero. The sign is not consistently negative, so H2 would not count as a finding on this day.
- **H2b (my hypothesis).** Drop in fill probability from 1 ms to 500 ms, back minus front, is 0.0005 with interval negative 0.0050 to 0.0059. No sign of an interaction on this day.
- **Surprise.** The gap between rules (about 22 points, 68.3% under front against 46.7% under back) is huge next to anything delay does. That fits Danait et al. qualitatively, though their gaps were measured on Tokyo equities and are not comparable in size.
- **Markout, secondary (H2c).** The 10 s maker markout of filled orders is negative under all three rules, about negative 0.4 bps (front), negative 0.7 (random), negative 1.0 (back) at 1 ms. At 500 ms the unconditional figures are within 0.03 bps of those.
- **Matched markouts run against H2c.** On decisions that fill at both 1 ms and 500 ms (5,388 under front, 4,283 under random, 3,708 under back), the 10 s markout is better at 500 ms than at 1 ms by 0.067 bps (front, negative 0.512 against negative 0.446), 0.062 bps (random) and 0.088 bps (back). The 1 s markouts move the same way, by about 0.034 bps under each rule. H2c predicts the opposite sign. I computed no bootstrap interval for these differences, so they are point estimates on one day. An interval goes into `passive.py` before the test days.
- **Across rules, exploratory.** Back rule fills lose more than front rule fills. That is consistent in direction with Yueshen's end of queue prediction, but it compares cancellation rules, not measured queue positions. Back rule fills are also a selected set, since they only happen after more volume trades at my price, so part of the gap is selection. It is not H2c, which is about delay.
- **Many orders lose their level.** At every delay, in 37.6% to 37.8% of decisions my price left the top five levels before the order had filled under all three rules, and from then on it counts as unfilled under any rule that had not yet filled. The results file does not split this by rule, so I cannot yet say how much of each rule's unfilled share it explains. It is a large part of why fill probability sits where it does.
- **Why flat delay is not a surprise.** An order that arrives later simply joins the queue as it stands then. In this design delay changes the queue it joins only through how the book moved in between. It cannot lose a place in line it never held. A real trader who would have joined earlier loses queue priority to others, and this replay has no other traders reacting to me. So the flat result is partly built in, not proof that delay does not matter for passive orders. With random decision times the arrival is just another random moment, so a near zero change in fill probability is what the design should give whatever the market does. The fixed price arm in the Deviations section is meant to fix this.
- **Limits and gaps.**
  - *Dev day only.* H2 is judged on two test days. This is a preview.
  - *Missing:* signal driven decisions. Random decisions carry no information, so adverse selection from a stale signal is absent.
  - *Fell short:* top of book and five levels only. Cancels are inferred from size drops, so a cancel and an add of equal size in one update cancel out, and a trade stamped in a different millisecond from its book update can be counted as a cancel or an add. I have not measured how often.
  - *Fell short:* the random rule places one lump per update. Real cancels are many small orders.
  - *Not checked:* the horizon is measured from arrival, not from the decision. Markouts use the fill price as reference. These choices are logged under Deviations.
  - *Resources that would help:* order level data (Tardis incremental book) to see real queue positions, and live decision timestamps.

## 2026-11-18 (Wed, afternoon) · Yueshen read in full
- **What I read.** The open access copy from the SMU repository, which is the Articles in Advance version (pages 1 to 20, online 17 September 2025). The issue citation is Management Science 72(6):4760-4779, 2026. I read the whole text and looked at all 22 PDF pages as laid out, because the equations and figures don't copy cleanly out of the PDF. The proofs I read for structure, not line by line. The online appendix is not in this copy and I have not read it.
- **What it is.** A one shot theory model of a one tick book. Makers submit at about the same time, latency decides only their order in the queue, and one investor then sends a market order. The only data is a descriptive TAQ figure. There is no price path after a fill, no markout horizon and no delay in milliseconds.
- **What holds from my earlier notes.** End of queue orders lose money in equilibrium (Lemma 1, Proposition 1), and units deeper in the queue are, conditional on filling, more likely to meet a more informed market order. Clustered submissions followed by quick cancellations, which in the model fall on the tail of new queues, is the paper's first prediction. The size ratio claim I removed this morning is in the paper after all. Proposition 4(i) says the cancel to add size ratio is below one when the sector mixes fast and slow makers, because the fast orders are larger and sit near the front, so cancellations come mostly from the smaller slow orders at the back. With identical makers the ratio goes to one. Proposition 4(ii) says it rises with the share of fast makers, while one sentence in the introduction reads the other way, so I cite Proposition 4 for the direction.
- **What does not hold.** The paper never says that an order sent later has a worse markout over a fixed horizon. That step, which is H2c and my 11-15 line about measuring what a late order earns, is my own inference from the end of queue result. In the paper being late means sitting behind competitors who posted in the same burst. In my replay a later order joins whatever queue stands then, with nobody reacting to it. So the dev day matched markouts, which run against H2c, do not contradict the paper. They test a mechanism it does not model. Logged under Deviations, with the test itself unchanged.
- **Limits and gaps.**
  - *Not read:* the online appendix (the microfoundation of the queue distribution and other demand setups).
  - *Approximate:* any figure value would be read off a chart. I quote none here.

- **What it is.** Theory with a rough calibration. One trader sells one share with a limit order whose price reaches the book a fixed latency late. The cost of latency is the spread she gives up by pricing on stale information, as a share of the cost of immediacy (Definition 1 and Corollary 1, p. 1078). There is no queue, no time priority, no competitor and no adverse selection, and the authors say so (pp. 1071, 1075).
- **What holds from my earlier notes.** The one line in the literature review was right as far as it went. The paper is model based, gives a closed form, is calibrated on equities, and has no split of drift against queue loss on book data. It goes further than that line said, since it has no queue at all. No claim of mine about it was wrong, so nothing is corrected.
- **What it changes.** Nothing in the preregistration. It gives a second reason, from the model side, for the 11-18 point that legs which reprice on arrival lose little on random decisions. In their terms such an order carries no stale information. The fixed price arm is the closer match to their setup.
- **Exploratory idea, not a registered test.** Their cost scales with the price move over the latency divided by the spread. On a one tick book that ratio could be large even at a few milliseconds, which would put their closed form outside its range. A later look could compare the shape of the fixed price cost against delay with that ratio. This is an idea only. It will not touch the test days and it does not change H1 to H5.
