## How Danait et al. (2026) changes my queue model
My plan for the passive leg used one queue rule. Queue ahead was the displayed size when my order arrives, and I fill once traded volume at my price exceeds it, with a pro rata cancellation variant as a side check. Their paper shows that this one choice can move passive fill rates by about 8 points on its own, on data with the same prices, sizes and trades. So a passive result from one rule would really be a result about the rule.

What I am changing, before any passive result exists.
1. Three matched cancellation rules. Front (cancels remove the oldest quantity, best case for me), quantity weighted random, and back (cancels remove the newest quantity, worst case). Same book path, same seeds, same trades for all three.
2. A passive latency effect only counts as a finding if its sign is the same under all three rules. If the sign flips between rules, I report that the data cannot decide it.
3. Every passive number is reported as a range across the three rules, not as one value.
4. Inside one book update I apply trades first, then cancellations, then additions, as they do, and my simulated order never changes the book.

My own inference, not in their paper. Delay and the cancellation rule may interact. Under the back rule, cancellations never shrink the queue in front of me, so the cost of arriving late comes mostly from quantity that joined ahead during the delay. Under the front rule, early cancellations can partly undo that loss. If so, the queue part of my latency cost could be smallest under front and largest under back. This is a hypothesis to test, not a result.

Limits of the transfer. Their numbers come from two Tokyo equities with proprietary data and zero latency. Binance BTCUSDT has a far larger queue relative to typical order size and a different tick regime, so I do not expect the same 8 point gap. Their front and back rules are endpoints of one reconstruction method, not true bounds on what the real queue did. LOBSTER has order IDs, so it may let me see where cancellations actually land in a queue and which rule is closer to reality. That is an idea, not yet checked.

## What is new here (claimed modestly)
1. A paired counterfactual design. Identical decision moments replayed at 1 to 500 ms of added delay (exchange timestamps are whole milliseconds, so 0 and 1 ms cannot be told apart), so the latency effect is not confounded by when slow orders happen to occur.
2. A decomposition of latency cost into price drift and queue position loss, on public data anyone can rerun. Planned, not built. The taker leg splits drift from arrival cost, and the passive leg has no queue loss term yet.
3. A test of whether latency cost is a tail phenomenon (RQ3) rather than a mean shift.

## Not found in this review
I did not find published work that replays the same decision across a controlled delay grid on public data. That says how far my search went, not that nobody has done it.

## What reading Albers et al. in full changed
I read the full text on 2026-11-15. They do not replay one decision across a controlled set of delays. Each of their orders has one realised latency, and they relate outcomes to measured latency, volatility and liquidity. So claim 1 above stands, as far as this review goes. Three things changed in my plan.
1. Their main result is about which orders slip (the ones that would have made money), not only how much. I added H1b to test whether my replay shows the same split. If it does not, that is a warning about the replay, not a new result.
2. They say a synthetic approach would need latency that depends on future returns. My replay keeps the real book path, which already contains other traders' reactions, but my order never races anyone in the same millisecond. I wrote that into the preregistration's limits and added a tie rule robustness check.
3. Their Binance arm is the BTC inverse perpetual over about an hour, not the USD-M contract I use. Their numbers are not a direct benchmark for mine.

Their tables and figures did not render in the HTML I read that morning. Later the same day I got the open access PDF from the Oxford repository (ORA) and read every table and the main figures. What the tables add.
1. Table 7 gives the slippage fits directly. Slope against markout is −0.0486 (Bybit BTC), −0.1526 (Binance BTC inverse) and −0.0337 (Bybit ETH), intercept 0. At a 2 bps markout that is about 0.10, 0.31 and 0.07 bps, which matches the numbers I took from their text for the H1 bar in the preregistration. The bar stands.
2. Table 6 fits failure rate against markout with weighted least squares, and the text says its R² is the weighted version. For the slippage fits in Table 7 the paper does not say, so I still do not call those weighted.
3. Table 4 puts their markout horizon at a median of 1.14 s (mean 1.48 s). The 1 s horizon in my H1b is close to theirs.
4. Table 1 lists the best public fees at the time. Binance USDT margined perpetual taker 1.7 bps, maker 0.0. So a 0.1 bps latency cost is small next to the fee, and I say that plainly when I report H1.
5. From a colocated server they put the time to reach the exchange at about 2 ms on average, standard deviation about 1 ms. My grid starts at 1 ms, so its low end sits where colocated traders already are.
